## H2: Answers to Open Questions

1.  **Recommendation for the weak territorial bucket (Q1): Choose option (a). Trust the revoker's recorded extent and classify the law as Part Revocation / Repeal.**
    **Reasoning:** This is the most risk-averse approach, aligning with the primary concern that a false "Revoked" status is the most damaging error.
    *   **Asymmetric Risk:** Classifying a law as `Part Revocation` when it might be fully `Revoked` creates, at worst, unnecessary review work. Classifying it as `Revoked` when it is still in force in one jurisdiction (e.g., Northern Ireland) hides live legal duties from customers in that region. The plan must prioritise safety.
    *   **Legislative Practice:** Territorial repeals by UK-level instruments are a standard feature of the UK's multi-jurisdictional legal system. It is common for a UK Statutory Instrument (UKSI) to apply only to Great Britain (GB), leaving Northern Ireland's law untouched. Similarly, a UK Public General Act (ukpga) may contain provisions to repeal prior legislation for England and Wales only, anticipating separate legislative action in Scotland and/or Northern Ireland. The Special Waste Regulations 1996 example is a classic case, not an anomaly. Therefore, when a UK-level revoker has an explicit extent narrower than the target law's extent, it is more likely to be a deliberate territorial repeal than a metadata error.
    *   **Data Integrity:** Option (a) makes data problems visible. By classifying these 400 cases as `Part Revocation` and recording the basis in `live_evidence`, you create a dataset of potential metadata inconsistencies that can be reviewed and corrected over time. Option (b) perpetuates the existing bug and hides these data quality issues. Option (c) is an arbitrary heuristic that adds complexity without a clear legal principle; the legal systems of Scotland and Northern Ireland are distinct, and there is no sound basis for trusting extent metadata for one but not the other.

2.  **Recommendation for "revoked with savings" and "revoked (prosp.)" (Q2): Your proposed handling is correct.**
    *   **"revoked with savings" should be treated as a full revocation.** A saving or transitional provision does not keep the legislative instrument itself in force. It preserves the legal effect of certain actions or rights created under that instrument *despite* its revocation. The instrument's status is unequivocally `Revoked`.
    *   **"revoked (prosp.)" should not be treated as a whole revocation.** A prospective revocation is one that is not yet in effect. The law remains fully in force until the commencement of the revoking provision. This should be treated identically to a revocation row where the "Applied" flag is "Not yet". The `live_evidence` field is the correct place to record the future nature of the revocation.

3.  **Recommendation for the recompute guard (Q3): The guard is well-designed but has one gap.**
    *   **The Gap:** The guard only triggers on laws where the *old rule* applied to *stored rows* reproduces the current `live` status. This misses laws that are currently `Revoked` but have no stored revocation rows. This could be due to a title marker override, a legacy data import, or a past data-loss event. The current plan would not touch their `live` status, but the description rewrite ("Revoked (source not recorded)") suggests you are aware of them.
    *   **Recommendation:** Explicitly add a condition to the guard: **Do not change the `live` status of any law that is currently `Revoked` but for which no revocation rows are stored.** This prevents these laws from being incorrectly flipped to `In force` by the recompute.

## H2: Gaps, Wrong Assumptions and Edge Cases

1.  **Gap: Aggregation of Multiple Territorial Revocations.** The plan correctly identifies that an Act can be revoked by different instruments in different jurisdictions (e.g., Special Waste Regs 1996). However, the logic to determine when the *sum* of these territorial revocations constitutes a *whole* revocation is not specified. The system must be able to track the remaining "in force" extent of a law and only change its status from `Part Revocation` to `Revoked` when all its jurisdictions are covered by revokers. For example, a UK Act revoked by an E+W SI, a Scottish SSI, and an NI Order is fully revoked. The `live_evidence` struct should explicitly list the remaining, un-revoked jurisdictions.

2.  **Wrong Assumption: Heuristic for "Whole Instrument".** The rule for identifying a whole revocation (`target` is empty, "whole instrument", etc.) is a good heuristic but may be fragile. A revoking instrument could effectively repeal an entire Act by listing every single section and schedule (e.g., "ss. 1-50 and Schs. 1-3 repealed"). Your current rule would miss this and classify it as a partial repeal. While this is a rare edge case, you should acknowledge this limitation of the pattern-matching approach.

3.  **Edge Case: Implicit Repeal.** The entire system is based on parsing explicit repeals listed in the "changes affected" table on legislation.gov.uk. It does not and cannot account for *implicit repeal* (also known as repeal by implication), where a later statute's provisions are so inconsistent with an earlier one that the earlier law is rendered void without being explicitly mentioned. This is a fundamental limitation of this methodology and should be documented as an assumption of the system.

## H2: Design Challenges

1.  **Challenge: The `live` Status Vocabulary is Now Insufficient.** The plan correctly identifies territorial revocations but forces them into the existing `Part Revocation / Repeal` bucket. This conflates two distinct concepts: a law where specific provisions are revoked everywhere, and a law that is fully in force but only in a subset of its original jurisdictions.
    *   **Recommendation:** For this iteration, mapping "territorial" to `Part Revocation` is a pragmatic, non-breaking choice. However, you should strongly consider introducing a fourth `live` status like `Territorially In Force` or `Regionally Revoked` in a future version. The new `kind` field (`territorial`) is an excellent first step, allowing you to build this functionality later without another full recompute. For now, ensure the `live_description` for these cases is unambiguous, e.g., "In force in Scotland; revoked elsewhere."

2.  **Challenge: `live_description` Generation Logic.** The plan to auto-generate a human-readable description is good, but the logic can become complex and brittle, especially with multiple territorial revokers. A law revoked in England by one SI and in Wales by another WSI at different times could lead to a convoluted description.
    *   **Recommendation:** Prioritise making the structured `live_evidence` field comprehensive and accurate. Keep the `live_description` string simple and stable. For complex cases, a generic description like "Revoked in some jurisdictions (see evidence for details)" is safer and more maintainable than a complex, auto-generated sentence. The structured data is the source of truth for downstream systems; the description is for quick human orientation.

3.  **Challenge: Revocation Date Granularity.** The plan uses the revoker's `md_date` as a proxy for the revocation date. This is often incorrect. The true date is the commencement date of the specific revoking provision, which can be years after the revoking law was made.
    *   **Recommendation:** Acknowledge this simplification. Using `md_date` is a reasonable starting point if commencement data is unavailable. The `live_evidence` field should label this date clearly (e.g., `"revoker_made_date": "..."`) to avoid misinterpretation as the precise date the revocation took effect. This flags an area for future data enrichment.
